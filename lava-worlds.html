Lava World and sub-Neptune Atmospheres

Where magma oceans meet atmospheres

I use the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) to study the atmospheres of two kinds of small planets that may house magma oceans: sub-Neptunes, whose hydrogen-dominated atmospheres may sit on top of a global magma ocean, and ultra-hot rocky “lava worlds” so close to their stars that they have molten surfaces. In both cases, exchange between the magma and the atmosphere can shape what we observe, and may provide a window into the earliest phase of Earth’s own history.

A sub-Neptune in its magma era

I am the principal investigator for the JWST Cycle 3 Program 6284, Searching for Signatures of Surface-Atmosphere Interaction on a Small Planet in its Magma Era. The program aims to determine whether interaction between a small planet’s hydrogen-rich atmosphere and its liquid magma interior can noticeably affect its atmospheric composition. We think that Earth went through a similar hydrogen-atmosphere–liquid-surface phase very early in its lifetime, which may have produced the water oceans that we see today. I am also principal investigator of a Las Cumbres Observatory observing proposal (2025A) to characterize starspots on its host star, HD 207496.

I am a co-investigator on three other JWST sub-Neptune programs, GO 7407 (PI: Matthew Nixon), GO 8004 (PI: Natasha Batalha), and GO 10047 (PI: Rafael Luque), listed below, observing a total of 11 other planets.

Ultra-hot lava worlds

So-called “lava worlds” are rocky planets so close to their stars that their daysides are likely molten. Previous observations from the Spitzer Space Telescope hinted that some of these planets might have thick atmospheres. This goes against our expectations that these planets should quickly lose their atmospheres due to high amounts of stellar radiation. I am the data analysis lead for JWST Program GO 4818 (PI: Megan Weiner Mansfield) observing 10 of these planets to see how these atmospheres may have formed. I led a study reporting an atmosphere on HD 3167 b, and contributed to a study finding a potential atmosphere on TOI-431b.

The way we probe whether these planets have atmospheres is by observing their dayside thermal emission (their temperature). The basic idea is that a thick atmosphere should move some heat from the dayside to the nightside through winds, resulting in a cooler dayside. However, if the planet has reflective clouds like Venus, these could further cool the dayside. I used TESS phase curves of small planets to reveal that these planets are much shinier than gas giant planets at similar temperatures.

I am also a co-investigator on JWST Cycle 4 GO 7875 (PI: Michael Zhang), which targets 55 Cancri e — the rocky exoplanet with the strongest, though still debated, evidence for an atmosphere — with three eclipse observations to test whether that atmosphere is real.
